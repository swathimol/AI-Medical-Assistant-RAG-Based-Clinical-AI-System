TYPE 2 DIABETES
Type 2 diabetes is a chronic condition in which the body does not use insulin properly (insulin resistance) and blood glucose rises. Common symptoms include increased thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, blurred vision, and slow-healing wounds. Many people have no symptoms early on. Risk factors include overweight or obesity, physical inactivity, family history, and age over 45. Diagnosis is made with blood tests such as fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, or an oral glucose tolerance test. An HbA1c of 6.5% or higher on two tests indicates diabetes. Management includes healthy eating, regular physical activity, weight management, blood glucose monitoring, and medicines such as metformin when prescribed by a clinician. Long-term complications include heart disease, kidney damage, nerve damage, eye disease, and foot problems. Regular check-ups help detect complications early.

HYPERTENSION
Hypertension, or high blood pressure, is a condition in which the force of blood against artery walls is consistently too high. Blood pressure is written as systolic over diastolic, for example 120/80 mmHg. In adults, a reading of 130/80 mmHg or higher on repeated measurements is generally considered high under American guidelines, while many other guidelines use 140/90 mmHg. Hypertension often has no symptoms, which is why it is called the silent killer. Very high readings may cause headache, shortness of breath, or nosebleeds. Untreated hypertension increases the risk of stroke, heart attack, heart failure, and kidney disease. Lifestyle measures include reducing salt intake, eating more fruits and vegetables, regular exercise, limiting alcohol, not smoking, and maintaining a healthy weight. Medicines are prescribed when lifestyle changes are not enough.

ASTHMA
Asthma is a chronic lung condition in which the airways become inflamed and narrow, making breathing difficult. Typical symptoms are wheezing, coughing (especially at night or early morning), chest tightness, and shortness of breath. Triggers include allergens such as pollen and dust mites, respiratory infections, cold air, exercise, smoke, and strong odors. Diagnosis involves medical history and lung function tests such as spirometry. Treatment usually includes quick-relief inhalers for sudden symptoms and long-term controller medicines such as inhaled corticosteroids for people with persistent asthma. An asthma action plan helps patients recognize worsening symptoms. Severe difficulty breathing, blue lips, or inability to speak in full sentences is an emergency and needs immediate medical care.

IRON DEFICIENCY ANEMIA
Iron deficiency anemia occurs when the body lacks enough iron to produce hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. Common symptoms include tiredness, weakness, pale skin, shortness of breath, dizziness, cold hands and feet, and brittle nails. Causes include inadequate dietary iron, blood loss (such as heavy menstrual periods or gastrointestinal bleeding), pregnancy, and poor iron absorption. Diagnosis uses a complete blood count and iron studies such as serum ferritin. Treatment includes iron-rich foods like red meat, beans, lentils, and leafy greens, iron supplements when recommended by a clinician, and treating the underlying cause of blood loss. Vitamin C can improve iron absorption, while tea and coffee taken with meals can reduce it.

COMMON COLD AND INFLUENZA
The common cold is a mild viral infection of the nose and throat. Symptoms include runny nose, sneezing, sore throat, cough, and mild fatigue, usually lasting 7 to 10 days. Influenza (flu) is caused by influenza viruses and tends to start suddenly with fever, chills, body aches, headache, cough, and marked tiredness. Flu can lead to complications such as pneumonia, especially in older adults, young children, pregnant women, and people with chronic illnesses. Both spread through respiratory droplets and contaminated surfaces. Rest, fluids, and symptom relief are the main treatments for colds. Antiviral medicines may be prescribed for flu if started early. Antibiotics do not work against viruses. Annual flu vaccination and regular handwashing reduce the risk of infection. Seek medical care for difficulty breathing, persistent high fever, chest pain, or confusion.

DENGUE FEVER
Dengue is a viral infection spread by Aedes mosquitoes, common in tropical and subtropical regions including India. Symptoms appear 4 to 10 days after the bite and include sudden high fever, severe headache, pain behind the eyes, muscle and joint pain, nausea, vomiting, and rash. Most cases are mild, but some progress to severe dengue, with warning signs such as severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding gums or nose, blood in vomit or stool, and restlessness. There is no specific antiviral treatment. Care involves rest, plenty of fluids, and paracetamol for fever. Aspirin and ibuprofen should be avoided because they can increase bleeding risk. Warning signs require urgent hospital care. Prevention focuses on eliminating standing water, using mosquito repellent, and wearing protective clothing.
